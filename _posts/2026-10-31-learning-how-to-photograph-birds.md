This weekend, I was out and about with my camera for the first time, learning how to photograph birds.
Birds are often small, unpredictable targets, and after sitting in the mud for half an hour on Saturday morning, I was beginning to wonder what I had gotten myself into!

One thing I am learning about wildlife photography is that in order to get good shots, you have to be in the right place at the right time. So there I was, 9am in the morning, positioned along riverbank on the water of Leith and waiting for a kingfisher. I saw a shock of vibrant blue feathers flying downstream, and then, once I had got myself into position with my camera, no kingfishers at all. The ground was wet from the rain the night before, and I wasn't sure whether to squat or sit. Even though I didn't quite know what I was doing, the river was peaceful and alive with birdsong, and I was glad to be there.

In the lack of kingfishers, and other birds, I decided instead to walk around and take photos instead. The morning light was reflecting the leaves in the river, and I was playing with the different settings on the camera to best capture the autumn colours. 

I then ventured out to Inverleith park, where I knew there would be plenty of ducks and a family of swans for me to photograph. There have been a pair of mute swans on the lake there, with a new arrival since March 2025. Now, the young cygnet has grown into a full adult, and during my time in Edinburgh, I've been watching her grow up. I visit the park at least once a month, and I really enjoyed getting my first few shots with the swan family. This swan is the cover photo for this blog post, and the swan family is shown below.

Fun fact: to tell apart male and female swans, look at the knob above their beaks. Males generally have larger knobs, as shown in the third photograph.

I also experimenting with some action shots, like with these mallards! In birdwatching, there is something called "giss". It stands for *general impression of size and shape*, and distills the characteristics of a bird. It includes both appearance and behaviour; it could be the round cheerfulness of a robin, or the sleek and skittish kingfisher.

This birdwatching trip had got me thinking about a new goal I wanted to achieve. I had set out, trying to capture an elusive kingfisher, and had come out with something very different in trying to capture the playfulness of the ducks. Perhaps, photographing birds is not always about finding the rarest species, but about capturing their unique and distinctive personalities (and trying not to get a muddy tush in the process).
